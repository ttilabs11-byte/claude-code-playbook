# YouTube growth (Jake Trinder system)

Core idea: a video is a series system. Topic, packaging, hook, structure, edit and CTA must all work, and five
decisions at 90% each succeed together only 59% of the time (0.9^5). Remove every single point of failure. The feed
is winner-take-all: about 20 videos compete and one gets the click, and YouTube pushes the video whose viewers are most
satisfied. Quality of decisions beats volume: one strong video can change a channel. Decisions are only as good as the
information behind them (bounded rationality), so research comes first, and interpretation comes from psychology and
first principles, never from copying.

## Procedure

### 0. Research (do once per niche, then keep it up)
Depth-first search, so nothing is missed:
1. Root = the niche (or an adjacent niche when hunting formats). Generate an exhaustive list of seed search terms.
2. Take one term, search it, and open the videos whose views are high for the channel's size.
3. Open that channel and log every outlier, because one outlier means there are usually more. Then go back to the
   term's next result. When results stop being relevant, move to the next term.
4. Outlier database, one row per video: title, channel, date, multiplier against the channel's median, whether that
   multiplier is significant (volatile channels spike by chance), niche, topic, and the hypothesis for why it won
   (title pattern, thumbnail style, execution format such as a 20-40 point list rather than 5-8 points).
5. Format database: the same framing winning across several niches. Score each format on median views, median
   multiplier, the last time it won, and its hit-to-miss ratio. Then ask whether it makes sense from first principles.
6. Log negative evidence too (the format tried and failing, and why). If you only research winners you can prove any
   bad idea. Old winners still count when nothing has changed in the psychology behind them: faded formats can be revived.

### 1. Idea: revealed preference
What people need and what they choose differ, so pick from what buyers already watch.
1. Search the problem your customer is trying to solve.
2. Find the floor (the median for a decent video) and the ceiling (the best videos). Is that range worth it?
3. Check the views are real and the demand is recent.
4. Raise the ceiling by stacking demand: attach the topic to a bigger name or trend nobody has combined with it yet
   ("Alex Hormozi's Lead Generation Strategy": 473k views).
5. Match the awareness level (Schwartz: problem, solution, product, most aware). Sell the outcome they want, and use
   your mechanism as the new reason to care ("If you don't understand insulin, you don't understand fat loss").
   A mechanism goes in the title only when the outcome carries the demand or the title questions a belief.
6. Test: "What evidence says the people I want as customers already choose to watch this?" No evidence, no video.
Gut check: a good idea makes you nervous about getting it wrong.

### 2. Packaging: title and thumbnail
- A format is a proven framing of a promise. One win is a clue, not a pattern. Copy the reason it worked, not the
  words (copying the words alone is overfitting: "$1 vs $10,000" fails for a B2B audience).
- Title: take the constant part from proven titles and swap in your variable. Confirm the reason transfers (in "If you
  don't understand X...", X must truly be unknown to this audience).
- Thumbnail: noticed and understood in one glance. About 3 elements, the desired outcome shown visually, a curiosity
  gap, authority cues (face, scrubs), strong contrast (white text on a red block), the audience matched. Thumbnail and
  title make one promise together.
- Go as broad as you can without losing resonance with the core buyer. Specificity costs reach, so pay for it only
  when the right person feels more understood.
- Simulate the feed next to live competitors. A/B test only when you are genuinely unsure: tests on small channels
  split momentum.

### 3. Hook (first 30 seconds) = 4P
Promise (this is what you clicked for), Proof (why trust me: one honed authority line reused on every video), Plan (the
steps I'll cover), Persona (this is for you, with the door left open for adjacent viewers). Then onboarding: an aha
moment within the next 30 seconds, so the viewer feels right to stay.

### 4. Body: acceptance criteria
The title is the spec. Ask what must be true by the end for the title to be true, then break each answer down to its
smallest truthful part. That list is the outline, and the video is done only when every condition passes. Optimise
satisfaction against expectation, not retention: Jake's 800k-view video had 11-12% average retention. Edit to educate
(cut fluff, diagram the hard parts) and match the edit to the promise: a raw promise needs a raw edit ("Speedrunning
Cold Calls... (Show Everything)", $100k in 30 days). Script fully for tight entertainment talking heads. Use an outline
for screen recordings and challenges. Get the creator's own IP out by interview, and use AI as a sparring partner,
never as the writer.

### 5. Monetise: give, don't take
- Value, value, then a random pitch reads as taking, and it costs satisfaction, reach and conversions.
- Product: build it into the video so it removes the exact constraint (Instantly's pre-warmed domains inside a
  10-hour challenge). Test: would an unbiased expert include it because it makes the video better?
- Service or info: extend the lesson with a free tool the viewer needs next (an ad library, a scorecard sheet).
- Implied CTA: tell how you get clients their results as an example, and interested viewers will find you.
- "How I" beats "How to" for revenue (about 2-3x) because it proves you've done it.
- Link CTR benchmarks: 1-2% for a pushy CTA, 5-10% for a give-first offer, 3.5-4% for a free group.

### 6. After publishing
- Leading signals: positive comment sentiment and the ratio of positive comments to views (the strongest signal),
  likes per view, subscribers per view.
- CTR and the retention curve tell you where to look, not what to do. A drop right after the hook means slow time to
  value. A dip mid-video is usually the CTA.
- Funnel: views, link CTR, landing page, opt-in, booking, show-up, close. Fix the narrowest point first. Look at both
  first-click and last-click attribution.
- The channel is one campaign. An off-topic viral hit brings viewers who never return. Split different audiences into
  separate channels.

### 7. Double down
Find why it won, keep that reason, and use the signal in a new way: combine formats or invent a new one. (Gary Vee:
"New Rules of Social Media" at 300k, then "Brutally Honest Advice..." at 700k, then the invented "The New Era of
Interest Media Has Just Begun" at 400k+.) Keep the execution format as well as the title: the "I made X and learned
this" videos won because each listed 20-40 points.

## Pitfalls
- Picking topics from what the expert thinks the audience needs.
- Copying an outlier from another niche without the reason it won.
- Treating CTR or retention as an instruction.
- Posting to keep a schedule without a goal for the video.
- Building a channel around one viral video.
- Flashy edits on a raw promise.
- A pitch that takes from the viewer.

## Verification (before a video or post ships)
- [ ] Demand evidence named: the outliers, their dates and their multipliers.
- [ ] The format and the reason it works are written down, and the reason transfers to this audience.
- [ ] Title and thumbnail make one clear promise, and the thumbnail reads in one glance next to live competitors.
- [ ] The hook covers all four Ps, with the aha moment inside the first minute.
- [ ] Every acceptance criterion for the title passes.
- [ ] The CTA gives rather than takes, or is implied.
- [ ] Post-publish review planned: comment sentiment first, then the funnel.
